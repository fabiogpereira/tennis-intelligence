# Statistical skeptic review

## Research #01 pivot implications

The original pressure risks remain relevant for Research #02. For Tennis DNA, the first statistical question is whether the proposed behavioral features are measured reliably enough to represent style at all. New risks include notation-parser error, denominator differences across features, unequal charting exposure, and circularity from selecting features after seeing player profiles.

Review target: the proposed state-based Pressure Performance Index (PPI). Status: adversarial pre-implementation review.

## Strongest arguments in favor
- **PROJECT HYPOTHESIS:** A counterfactual change in match-win probability is more granular than fixed labels such as break point because it varies with score, format, surface, and relative strength.
- Point leverage has an interpretable unit: expected change in eventual match-win probability.
- Conditioning expected point performance on server, receiver, surface, era, and strength could separate context from raw outcomes.
- A time-forward persistence test can make the question empirically falsifiable.

These are reasons to investigate, not evidence that the metric works.

## Strongest objections
1. **Model dependence:** Leverage is generated by a win-probability model. A misspecified model can manufacture importance and pressure residuals.
2. **Post-treatment and leakage:** Features derived from final rankings, future matches, or the observed point outcome can leak information into forecasts.
3. **Player quality confounding:** Better players may face different states and win more high-leverage points without a distinct pressure response.
4. **Opponent and surface confounding:** A player's schedule and surface mix can make apparent clutch estimates non-comparable.
5. **Selection effects:** Break points and late-set points are not randomly assigned. Tactics, serve choices, fatigue, and score effects affect who reaches them.
6. **Small effective samples:** High leverage points are rare and clustered within matches. Naive point counts overstate independent evidence.
7. **Regression to the mean:** Selecting the best-looking player-season guarantees some decline even without a real change.
8. **Circularity:** If the same points train the expected-performance and leverage models, the residual is not an honest test.
9. **Interpretation drift:** A predictive residual is not automatically a psychological measure of composure or character.
10. **Multiple comparisons:** Many players, surfaces, seasons, leverage bands, and model variants create false discoveries unless pre-specified.

## Simpler explanations to test
- Player strength alone explains the result.
- Serve/return quality and surface explain it.
- Late-score tactics or fatigue explain it.
- A match-specific random effect explains it.
- The estimate is a selection artifact from noisy periods.

## Candidate approaches

### A. Leverage-weighted residual performance
Estimate calibrated point win probability `p_expected`, match-win leverage `L`, and model the outcome residual as a player effect whose contribution is weighted by `L`.

Interpretability: high. Statistical validity: depends heavily on both models and cross-fitting. Data: point-by-point plus reliable match state. Bias susceptibility: high without controls. Complexity: high. Product usefulness: high if uncertainty is prominent.

### B. Hierarchical high-leverage outcome model
Model point outcomes directly with player, opponent, surface, era, server, state, and a leverage interaction, using partial pooling for player effects. Estimate whether player-specific pressure interactions differ from zero.

Interpretability: medium-high. Statistical validity: stronger handling of uncertainty and shrinkage, but still model-dependent. Data: point-by-point and covariates. Bias susceptibility: medium-high. Complexity: high. Product usefulness: medium-high.

### C. Matched or stratified pressure comparison
Compare outcomes in high-leverage states with similar low-leverage states, stratified by player/opponent/surface/era and evaluated out of sample. Treat the contrast as an association, not a latent trait.

Interpretability: very high. Statistical validity: transparent but vulnerable to residual confounding and sparse strata. Data: point-level states. Bias susceptibility: medium. Complexity: medium. Product usefulness: high for an explanatory first release.

## Comparison and recommendation
No winner is selected in Phase 1. Candidate C is the clearest baseline; B is the strongest candidate for uncertainty-aware player effects; A is the most direct expression of the original idea but also the easiest to overinterpret. A sensible study can benchmark all three against a no-pressure player-quality model.

## Validation framework
1. Freeze an analysis specification before looking at holdout results.
2. Build chronological train/validation/test splits by match date; keep all points from a match together.
3. Fit expected point and win-probability models using training data only; use cross-fitting for residuals.
4. Evaluate Brier score, log loss, calibration curves, calibration slope/intercept, and state-stratified performance.
5. Compare each pressure model to player-quality, opponent-quality, surface, era, serve/return, and match-random-effect baselines.
6. Use hierarchical shrinkage or uncertainty intervals; report effective match counts, not only point counts.
7. Test sensitivity to leverage definition, model family, minimum sample, surface, format, and era.
8. Test persistence by ranking or estimating player effects in period A and evaluating in later period B, with disjoint matches and opponents where feasible.
9. Use permutation or placebo tests that preserve match clustering and score-state structure.
10. Report null and negative results, rank stability, and calibration before any product label.

## Human judgment required
The project owner must decide what estimand the public product wants: prediction, descriptive association, or a claim about a persistent player characteristic. Those are different studies and must not be collapsed into one score.
